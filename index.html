FuseBox Detective

A calm, step-by-step way to record what trips your lights or power and narrow down what an electrician may need to check.

No tools • No fuse-box covers • No electrical repairs
Safety comes firstIf you notice a burning smell, smoke, crackling, unusual heat, scorch marks, electric shocks, or water near electrical equipment, stop. Do not reset the breaker or try more tests. Keep away and contact an electrician urgently; call emergency services if there is a fire or immediate danger.

1What happened?

Start with the symptom you have already noticed. You can use this guide without touching the fuse box.

2Record the switches — don't keep testing

Use this list to record what you already know, or what happened during ordinary use. Do not deliberately keep switching a suspect circuit on and off to provoke a trip. If it trips, leave that circuit off if you can do so safely and arrange an electrician.

Stop testing now

Do not switch the affected lights back on or reset the breaker. Keep clear of the equipment. For smoke or fire, leave the area and call emergency services. Otherwise seek urgent advice from a qualified electrician.

3Your likely next step

This is a guide to what to report, not a diagnosis of the wiring.

Likely area to investigate

Because the hallway/stairway lights appear to trigger the trip, report that specific lighting circuit and the exact switch or light involved. A faulty lamp, light fitting, switch, wiring, or moisture could be responsible. Only an electrician can safely identify the cause.

4Quick diagnosis: useful clues before paying for another visit

These checks are observations only. They cannot prove which part is faulty, but a clear timeline and a few specific questions can help an electrician focus their investigation.

A light trips the power after several minutes

A delayed trip is important information, but it does not prove that the bulb itself is faulty. A problem may develop as equipment warms up, or the trip may be caused by another fault on the same protected circuit.

  • Write down the timing. For example: “Hallway lights were on for about 10 minutes, then the power tripped.” Note what else was already running. Do not switch things on again just to reproduce it.
  • Record what was checked. If bulbs, fittings or a Bluetooth bulb have already been looked at, tell the electrician what was checked and what remains uncertain. A visual check alone may not rule out a wiring or insulation fault.
  • Mention other devices without assuming they are to blame. If a cheap plug-in fan was suspected, say whether it was running and which power went off. A plug-in fan may be unrelated; an electrician can establish whether it shares the affected circuit or protective device.
  • Note which switch moved, only if you can see it safely. A small circuit breaker (MCB) and a wider RCD/RCCB can indicate different kinds of fault. Do not remove the consumer-unit cover or touch wiring.
Ask the electrician: “Could you identify which circuit and protective device tripped, test the hallway/stairway lighting circuit and its switches/fittings for faults, and tell me what you found? If an appliance is suspected, can you confirm how it relates to the circuit rather than guessing?”

A short written record may save time on a follow-up visit, but no checklist can guarantee a cheaper repair or replace proper electrical testing. If there is a burning smell, crackling, scorching, unusual heat, damp or any shock/tingling, stop using the affected circuit and seek urgent professional help.

What the trip might mean

  • One lighting circuit trips: the fault may be on that circuit, for example a fitting, switch, cable, or moisture-related issue.
  • An RCD trips: it may be detecting current leaking to earth. Several circuits or an appliance can sometimes be involved.
  • A breaker trips when a particular light is used: the timing is useful evidence, but it does not prove the switch or bulb itself is the cause.
  • More than one circuit or the main switch trips: report exactly what went off; don't assume it is simply an overloaded fuse.

Never remove a fuse-box cover, touch wiring, open a light fitting, or remove bulbs to investigate a trip. Don't repeatedly reset a breaker or bypass a safety device. In the UK, use a suitably qualified electrician (for example, one registered with NICEIC or NAPIT).

FuseBox Detective is an educational checklist, not an electrical test or substitute for professional inspection. It does not connect to your fuse box and stores no information on a server; entries remain in this page until you close or reset it.
